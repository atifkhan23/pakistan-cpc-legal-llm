### Reference

Under Rule 15, before issuing any commission under Order XXVI, the court may order such sum as it thinks reasonable for the expenses of the commission to be paid into court by the party at whose instance or for whose benefit the commission is issued, within a time to be fixed by the court. This is a precondition to the issuance of the commission - the court has discretion whether to impose this requirement.

Where expenses have not been deposited, the commissioner can recover the amount from the party at whose instance he was engaged per A 1923 C 436.

## COMPARATIVE (id 172)

**Question:** Compare an appeal and a review under Section 96 and Section 114 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 with regard to their nature, grounds, and forum.

### Reference
Sections 96 and 114 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 provide for two distinct remedies against judicial decisions: appeal and review respectively. Pakistani courts have drawn careful distinctions between these two remedies.

As to nature and forum: review proceedings take place before the same court which passed the order or decree sought to be reviewed, while an appeal always lies before a superior court, as confirmed in PLD 1972 SC 279, PLD 1973 N 14, and 10 CU 9. An appeal is a continuation of the original proceeding before a superior tribunal, as established in 2013 SCMR 1707 and PLD 2012 SC 1. A review is not a continuation of the proceeding, as held in 1986 SCMR 965 and PLD 1974 L 545.

As to grounds: an appeal under Section 96 lies on any ground of fact or law, without restriction in the case of a first appeal. A review under Section 114 is only allowed in the limited circumstances specified in Order 47 rule 1, namely discovery of new and important evidence, account of some mistake or error apparent on the face of the record, or for any other sufficient reason. The grounds for review are thus far more restricted than those for appeal, as held in PLD 1975 SC 47 and PLD 1962 SC 325.

As to nature of right: an appeal is a right of the party while a review is a power of the court, as confirmed in PLD 1971 L 59. The right of appeal is a substantive right, as established in 2005 SCMR 1785 and PLD 1981 SC 553. Revisional jurisdiction and review are more in the nature of privileges or discretionary powers.

As to concurrent availability: the remedies under Sections 12, 96, and 114 against an ex parte decree are concurrent and may be continued so long as a decision is not given in any of them, as held in PLD 1970 SC 196. The determination of any of these may render the other remedies infructuous if the decree against which they were directed is superseded.

Time spent in prosecuting a review application can be excluded under Section 5 of the Limitation Act 1908 in calculating limitation for appeal, as held in PLD 1970 SC 196.

- The appellate court can either affirm, reverse, or modify the decision of the lower court.

**Review (Section 114):**
- A review is a mechanism to correct errors or irregularities in the proceedings of a lower court after the judgment has been delivered.
- It is more informal and less formal than an appeal.
- The purpose is to rectify mistakes made during the trial rather than to re-evaluate the merits of the case.

### Grounds

**Appeal (Section 96):**
- Grounds for an appeal include:
  - Errors of law.
  - Misapprehension of facts.
  - Inadequate or insufficient evidence.
  - Improper application of law.
  - Omission to apply a relevant law.
  - Excess or omission in the exercise of discretion.
- Appeals can be filed against final judgments, interlocutory orders, and certain other orders as specified by the CPC.

**Review (Section 114):**
- Grounds for a review include:
  - Errors in the appreciation of evidence.
  - Omissions or mistakes in the record of the proceedings.
  - Discovery of new evidence that could not have been discovered before the trial.
  - Irregularities in the conduct of the trial.
  - Any other ground that would justify a review.
- Reviews can be sought against any order passed by a court, including interim orders, but they cannot be used to challenge the final judgment on its merits.

### Forum

**Appeal (Section 96):**
- Appeals are filed in the appellate court, which is usually a higher court than the one that rendered the original judgment.
- The appellate court will hear the appeal and may allow oral arguments if necessary.
- The appellate court can refer the matter back to the lower court for further action if required.

**Review (Section 114):**
- Reviews are filed in the same court that passed the order against which the review is sought.
- The court that hears the review is the same court that made the original order, and it can either uphold the order or set it aside.
- The court may also refer the matter back to the trial court for further proceedings if necessary.

### Summary

- **Appeals** are directed towards challenging the correctness of the decision on points of law and fact, and they are heard by a higher court.
- **Reviews** are aimed at correcting errors or irregularities in the proceedings of the lower court, and they are heard by the same court that made the original order.

Both appeals and reviews serve important functions in the judicial system, ensuring that justice is fair and that errors are corrected. However, they differ significantly in their nature, grounds, and the forum in which they are heard.

## CONSEQUENCE (id 401)

**Question:** What is the procedural consequence when a plaint is returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC for presentation to the proper court?

### Reference
Order VII Rule 10 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 provides that where a plaint is presented to a court that has no jurisdiction - whether territorial, pecuniary, or subject-matter jurisdiction - the court shall return the plaint for presentation to the proper court. The procedural consequences of this return are significant and require careful attention to limitation.

Upon return under Rule 10, the court endorses the plaint with: (a) the date of presentation; (b) the date of return; (c) the name of the party presenting the plaint; and (d) a brief statement of the reason for return. This endorsement is crucial because it establishes the date the plaint was first filed - relevant for computing whether the period during which the proceeding was pending in the wrong court can be excluded from the limitation period under Section 14 Limitation Act 1908.

The Lahore High Court in 2010 CLC 1456 Lahore held that the plaintiff who receives the returned plaint must present it to the proper court within the limitation period. If the limitation period had not expired when the plaint was returned, the plaintiff must refile before expiry. Where the limitation period has expired since the original filing, Section 14 of the Limitation Act allows the plaintiff to exclude the period spent in the wrong court - provided they were prosecuting the claim with due diligence in good faith.

The Supreme Court of Pakistan in PLD 1977 SC 230 set out the requirements for Section 14 benefit: (1) the court must have been unable to entertain the suit due to defect of jurisdiction; (2) the plaintiff must have been prosecuting the suit with due diligence; and (3) the plaintiff must have acted in good faith - an honest mistake as to which court had jurisdiction qualifies.

The Sindh High Court in 2013 CLC 789 Karachi confirmed that a plaintiff who promptly refiles in the correct court - relying on the Rule 10 endorsement and Section 14 - will not be barred by limitation due to the time spent in the wrong court.
